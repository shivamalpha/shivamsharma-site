AI strategy · Investment

Where should the next AI dollar go?

Most enterprises do not have an idea shortage. They have a prioritization problem.

Shivam Sharma · September 2026

The question

How do you choose between another use case, shared AI capabilities and the less visible work required to make adoption stick?

A long list of AI use cases can create the impression of strategy. It is usually only a list.

The harder decision is where the next unit of investment should go. Another customer-facing copilot may have a cleaner demo than identity, evaluation, data access or workflow integration. But the second category can determine whether ten use cases scale or ten pilots remain ten pilots.

Start with the business problem

I would not rank AI opportunities primarily by how impressive the model looks. Start with the value of the problem, how often it occurs, how much of the workflow AI can actually influence and whether the organization is prepared to change the way the work gets done.

This also filters out point solutions looking for a problem. A technically feasible use case can still be a poor investment if the workflow is marginal, adoption is unlikely or the cost of integrating it exceeds the value created.

Use cases should shape the platform

The answer is not to swing to the other extreme and build a large AI platform in anticipation of every future need. Shared capabilities should be pulled by a portfolio of real use cases.

If several important use cases need secure model access, evaluation, identity, retrieval, observability or tool integration, those capabilities start to earn a place in the platform. The architecture should become reusable because demand repeats, not because a reference diagram says every box is mandatory.

The portfolio should pull the platform forward. The platform should make the next use case cheaper to deliver.

Count the constraints before counting the upside

In production, the constraint is often outside the model. Security approval can stall deployment. On-premise infrastructure can struggle with production load. Data access may be fragmented. A workflow may have no clear owner. An agent may perform well in a demo but fail on the exceptions that experienced employees handle almost without thinking.

These are not reasons to avoid AI. They are reasons to include execution risk in the investment decision instead of treating it as a problem for the implementation team later.

Do not confuse model sophistication with value

Enterprises can also spend too early on fine-tuning, bespoke infrastructure or increasingly complex agent designs. Sometimes those choices are justified. Often the problem can be solved with a capable model, good context, well-designed tools and tighter evaluation.

The strategic question is not whether the organization can build the more sophisticated system. It is whether sophistication buys enough additional business value, control or economics to justify itself.

Fund the bottleneck

If I had to reduce the decision to one rule, it would be this: fund the constraint preventing the next set of valuable use cases from working.

Sometimes that is a use case with a clear economic return. Sometimes it is a shared capability. Sometimes it is security, data, integration or evaluation. And sometimes the right decision is to stop funding an attractive pilot whose path to adoption is weak.

That is why AI investment cannot be separated neatly into strategy on one side and architecture on the other. The business portfolio tells you which capabilities matter. The technology constraints tell you which ambitions are credible. Good prioritization has to see both.
